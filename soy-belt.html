Teleconnected moisture

Flying Rivers

Some of the rain that grows soy in southern Brazil and the La Plata basin is made upwind, over the Amazon. This page follows it back, and shows where the evidence stops.

1 corridor · RECON 2008–2017 · every figure carries its denominator

Where the water starts

A declared source box in the western Amazon.

We start from a fixed box of the western Amazon, about 51 million hectares between 4 and 8 degrees south. Its forests return water to the air as vapour, and part of that vapour travels south toward the soy belt. The farms downwind have never had to account for where that water comes from.

The flying river

About a fifth of the moisture that leaves the source box reaches the soy belt.

We take the moisture flow from RECON, a published moisture-tracking dataset, and reconcile it to ERA5, the European reanalysis that reconstructs the global atmosphere hour by hour. Running our own tracking model (WAM2layers) is the next milestone, not a claim we make today. Of the moisture we track leaving the Amazon source box, about 20 percent falls inside the soy-belt box (RECON annual mean, 2008–2017). That is a share of what leaves the source, not a share of the rain that falls on the farms.

The reframe

A pipe has one source. This has thousands, and their positions are not equal.

We have been drawing one lane, as if the rain had a single headwater you could fence off. Underneath that lane is the real object: the same reconciled moisture flows that give the corridor its number are a directed, weighted network across South America, thousands of cells that each drink rain and breathe some back, wired to the next. A source forest is not a patch of green in that picture. It is a position. Two forests that look identical from the road can sit in completely different places in the wiring, and the wiring, not the canopy, is what sets how much of the belt's rain leans on them. So the useful question stops being where does the water start, and becomes which positions carry the load.

The keystone field

Read it off the graph: the supply is concentrated.

Point that network at one downwind economy, the South American soy grown in the Amazon-influenced rainfall system, and ask the inverse question the hub maps never ask: which upwind cells supply this belt's moisture, and how much. The dependency comes back concentrated, not spread. The top tenth of source cells carry about 48 percent of the moisture the belt receives from the tracked field, and it is not just the biggest evaporators doing the work, because the field barely tracks how much a cell evaporates. Reduced back to the corridor, the field returns the same annual weight, w = 0.2034. That is a consistency check, not an independent test.

The honest reading

Concentrated, yes. A hidden discovery, no.

We ran the field against its own null models before believing it, and it is worth showing what held and what did not. The concentration is real, and it is not an artifact of which cells evaporate the most. But most of it is the plain geometry of a corridor between a source and a sink: a shuffle that only preserves each cell's distance from the belt nearly reproduces the same concentration, and against equal-area patches drawn at random the field sits close to the middle of the pack. So read this as a sharper map of where the load already sits, not a new law of nature. The honest verdict is decision-useful, not Nature-grade, and we would rather say that plainly than let a clean map imply a precision it does not have.

The keystones hold

The keystones hold. Only the load breathes.

The cells that carry the load do not change with the seasons, but how much they carry does. Three moisture models agree on the order of the keystone cells, while the transport swings about 3.3 times between seasons: w is about 0.33 in the wet season and 0.10 in the dry. The seasonal field still reconciles to the committed annual value, w = 0.2034, within 1.5 percent.

The test

In 2022 the rivers ran thin. Our model got the direction and missed the size.

The La Niña drought of 2021 and 2022 cut Rio Grande do Sul's soy by more than half (IBGE), while the cerrado (Brazil's central savanna) held steady. The map shows what was observed. For Rio Grande do Sul our engine predicted a yield anomaly of about −4 percent, against about −44 percent observed. Across 22 harvests and 13 states the rainfall–yield link is modest (r ≈ 0.21), below the 0.30 bar we set ourselves, so the financial step stays closed. This is a backtest, not a forecast.

The missed map

Not every soy hectare carries the same drought load.

There is no price here. This map asks where soy value, drought history, heat pressure, and soil constraints overlap. It is useful for triage, but the benchmark did not promote it, so it sits beside the corridor analysis, not inside it.

The grade

A degraded forest may make the same rain, less reliably.

Here is a mechanism we take seriously, shown as an illustration. Published work documents intact, diverse forest holding its transpiration through drought better than degraded forest under the same heat. If that holds here, a forest's condition would change how steadily it makes rain more than how much it makes on average. We have not measured that link for this corridor. The layer that would track it is deferred until it passes its own tests, so nothing else on this page depends on it.

Where this goes

No price today. No forecast either.

Today the engine screens one corridor: a moisture attribution that passes its physical checks, and a drought backtest whose link to the harvest is still too weak to carry a price. Next, we run our own WAM2layers moisture ensemble and test the response step again. The same moisture map is already re-read on two Andean water towers, asking how much of Quito's and Bogota's rain arrives from the Amazon. We also watch the source: the 2023 and 2024 droughts drove its evaporation sharply below normal (TerraClimate, observed). That record sits beside the corridor, never inside it. A season-ahead warning is a research path, not a claim: Gate F has not run, and we will publish the skill before we ever call anything a forecast. The direction is a graph, not a lane. The same load-bearing-node method extends from this one corridor toward a directed precipitationshed network, and it already runs on other systems: our Upper Colorado corridor applies the identical method to a river basin, and it is an honest sibling, because it reports where the load is not concentrated as readily as where it is.
